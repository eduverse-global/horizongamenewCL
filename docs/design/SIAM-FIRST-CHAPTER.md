# Horizon — Siam chapter design

## Draft 0.1: A Letter Against the Tide

**Status:** planning document for review. This draft does not authorize implementation or change the running game. Character names, plot details, mechanics, timings, and numerical values below are design proposals, not historical claims or tested balance.

**Player promise:** explore a living Siamese river port, form an unlikely partnership, and use your knowledge of people and waterways to bring a threatened passenger boat safely to Ayutthaya.

**First playable target:** approximately 45–60 minutes on a first visit, including the optional exploration branch. Support saving and stopping throughout. Quality is measured by clarity, atmosphere, meaningful decisions, and reliable play—not the size of the map.

## 1. Direction and boundaries

### Agreed direction

- Narai-era Siam gives the game its central identity; the relationship with France under Louis XIV helps frame the wider campaign.
- Preserve the close-up HD-2D presentation: directional pixel characters, detailed 3D environments, illustrated portraits, and restrained interface panels.
- Towns are places to walk, investigate, trade, and meet people. Buildings have distinct purposes.
- Voyages should feel longer and more substantial, with larger playable regions and discoveries between destinations.
- Combat should reward strategy and varied objectives.
- Several protagonists pursue personal stories, cross paths before the finale, and contribute distinct abilities to a shared conclusion.
- Sacred-site exploration should offer stories, knowledge, and encounters with communities.

### Working recommendations, still open to review

- Use 1685–1688 as the broad campaign window; place this fictional opening around 1685, with its exact month unresolved.
- Grounded historical adventure with ambiguous folklore. No confirmed supernatural combat abilities in the first chapter. The campaign's ultimate level of fantasy remains undecided.
- Six eventual protagonists; only two receive playable opening chapters in the first release of this slice.
- Turn-based vessel tactics as the combat foundation. Detailed boarding combat is deferred.
- Historical public events remain anchors. Fictional protagonists change personal outcomes and local consequences; an alternate-history ending would require a separate decision.

## 2. Historical foundation and research work

Ayutthaya was a major capital and international center, with foreign trading and missionary communities. Its waterways should shape movement and settlement design. Reconstruct a living city rather than copying the appearance of today's ruins. [UNESCO: Historic City of Ayutthaya](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/576)

Kosa Pan's delegation, sent by King Narai, received an audience with Louis XIV on 1 September 1686. That is a verified campaign anchor; the altered letter, protagonists, and conflict in this chapter are fictional. [Thai Ministry of Foreign Affairs: Siamese envoy to France](https://www.mfa.go.th/en/content/108525-celebration-of-the-333rd-anniversary-of-the-siamese-envoy-to-france?menu=5d5bd3da15e39c306002aafa&page=5d5bd3da15e39c306002aaf9)

Use Bang Kok as the opening river gateway, Ayutthaya as the destination, and reserve Lopburi for later court stories. Detailed placement and date-specific architecture are research tasks, not yet approved reconstructions.

Before final historical art and dialogue, verify:

| Subject | Decision the research must support |
| --- | --- |
| River maps and settlements | A plausible route, navigable vessel sizes, landing places, and checkpoint placement |
| Fortifications and buildings | What existed in the chosen year; avoid importing later Bangkok landmarks |
| Clothing, names, occupations, and forms of address | Credible fictional people without making every community a single stereotype |
| Embassy procedures and language work | Who could handle which documents, and how an assistant might notice a discrepancy |
| Vessels, weapons, and crews | Plausible river craft and equipment; label compressed combat rules as game abstractions |
| Sacred-site practice and architecture | How visitors behave, who cares for the place, and what can appropriately be examined |
| Regional and international travel | Seasonality, historical routes, access, and plausible in-world duration |

Keep a source register separating contemporary accounts, later scholarship, modern heritage descriptions, and artistic invention. Seek Thai-language historical and cultural review before presenting the chapter as historically grounded. The two sources above establish context, not every proposed detail.

## 3. The protagonists

Working names are placeholders pending a naming and period review. Neither character represents a documented historical person.

| | Niran — river pilot | Mali — interpreter's assistant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting situation | Keeps a small family transport business afloat after a debt dispute | Copies commercial correspondence under an experienced interpreter |
| Immediate desire | Complete a well-paid passenger job without losing the boat | Find out why the translation of a passage differs from its original |
| Personal fear | Taking a principled risk will cost the crew their livelihood | Accusing the wrong person will destroy her mentor's reputation |
| Exploration ability | Read currents, inspect moorings, recognize navigational marks | Compare documents, ask precise questions, notice inconsistencies |
| Combat contribution | Identify safe shallow routes and improve maneuver planning | Recognize a false signal and attempt one evidence-based parley |
| First-chapter growth | Begins treating the passenger as a person rather than cargo | Learns when evidence needs allies to become effective |

Introduce Niran first, then briefly switch to Mali at a clearly marked chapter beat. They meet before departing. After joining, players can switch the lead character at safe moments; both accompany the voyage. Dialogue offers their distinct responses without requiring a character swap for every line.

The other proposed campaign roles—merchant, physician, engineer, and navigator—remain outlines. Existing prototype characters and quests remain available in the current build until a separate campaign integration decision is made.

## 4. Story and pacing

The disputed document is a fictional covering letter about an escort and delivery arrangement. It is not a fabricated quotation attributed to Narai or Louis XIV. Its alteration diverts a passenger and supporting records into a private interception. The culprits are a small fictional network with individual motives, not an entire nationality or religion.

| Beat | Player activity | First-play target | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. A boat to keep | As Niran, inspect the vessel, speak to the crew, and accept the passenger job | 7 min | Understand movement, interaction, and the pilot's stakes |
| 2. Two versions | As Mali, compare the letter with an archive copy and question a clerk | 7 min | Find an inconsistency and choose how openly to challenge it |
| 3. Before the tide | Meet at the dock, prepare supplies, and choose an initial route | 5 min | The protagonists cooperate; departure has a purpose |
| 4. Upriver | Navigate three short river segments with a checkpoint and a crew conversation | 10 min | Learn currents, make a route decision, and discover signs of pursuit |
| Optional: The unlit landing | Help at a fictional riverside sanctuary and inspect a damaged travelers' record | +8 min | Gain context, local trust, and a useful route clue |
| 5. The interception | Protect the passenger boat in a tactical encounter | 9 min | Escape, disable the attackers, or expose the false interception order |
| 6. The letter delivered | Reach a small Ayutthaya landing scene and resolve the immediate job | 5 min | Receive payment, see consequences, and discover the next lead |

Baseline is about 43 minutes; the optional branch brings it to about 51. These are playtest hypotheses, not enforced timers. Replay and accelerated travel should be shorter.

### Choices with visible consequences

- **Question the clerk publicly:** more people hear the discrepancy, but the pursuers are warned. The battle forecast shows them positioned farther upriver.
- **Quietly take a copy:** retain documentary evidence for the final handover; the clerk remains available as a later contact.
- **Help at the sanctuary:** spend extra time, earn a local endorsement, and learn a shallow bypass. Skipping it must not make the required battle unwinnable.
- **Disable an attacker:** recover evidence or salvage, but spend additional turns under pressure.
- **Prioritize escape:** protect the passengers and shorten the fight, leaving the pursuers' employer uncertain.

No arbitrary moral score. Track named facts such as evidence preserved, passengers safe, clerk alerted, sanctuary trust, and antagonist escaped. Show their immediate effect and reserve only a few for future chapters.

The ending resolves this delivery. A matching mark on another document suggests a broader pattern, giving both characters a reason to continue together. Avoid ending the entire demo on an unanswered cliffhanger.

## 5. Locations and walkable layout

Build one substantial Bang Kok district, one short river route with branches, one optional sanctuary, and one compact Ayutthaya arrival area. A complete second city and Lopburi are outside this slice.

The following is a gameplay layout, not a surveyed historical map:

```text
Homes and workshops — Market lane — Customs office
        |                  |               |
Shipwright — River promenade — Interpreter's rooms
        |                  |               |
Service landing — Passenger quay — Departure mooring
                           |
                    Lower river segment
                           |
              Checkpoint / shallow bypass
                           |
                  Optional sanctuary
                           |
                  Interception reach
                           |
                    Ayutthaya landing
```

Town traversal targets: 30–60 seconds between neighboring destinations, with a landmark, visible activity, or interaction opportunity along the way. Expand through authored lanes and waterfronts, not by stretching the existing streets. The complete district should remain understandable from its dock, market, and skyline.

Six building functions: provisions, repairs, passenger recruitment, document investigation, customs negotiation, and household stories. Only the spaces needed by the chapter require interiors; locked decorative doors should never imply unfinished required content.

Preserve four-direction character movement, click-to-walk, touch controls, and foreground occlusion handling. On-foot scenes use a following camera. Navigation panels open on demand rather than permanently covering the playfield.

## 6. Voyages that earn their length

Separate three layers: world chart for destination planning, regional sailing for navigation and events, and on-foot locations for detailed exploration. Retain global geography as a planning overview; the regional scene supplies detail that the current coarse ocean graph cannot represent.

| Journey category | Initial play-time hypothesis | Design obligation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nearby stop | 2–5 min | At least one navigational or social decision |
| Regional voyage | 8–15 min | Several distinct reaches, an optional stop, and character development |
| Major ocean crossing, later campaign | 20–30 min split into stages | Safe stopping points, meaningful events, and control over repetition |

The Bang Kok–Ayutthaya journey is the regional pacing experiment. Its main-route traversal and events occupy roughly ten minutes; the tactical battle is budgeted separately. No promise of a literal distance-to-minutes conversion.

- At route planning, show estimated play time, estimated in-world time, supplies, known hazards, and places to stop. Derive both time estimates from separate models.
- On familiar safe reaches, allow optional 2× or 4× acceleration. Stop before an encounter, landing, or hazard needing a decision.
- Allow unhurried 1× travel, pause, and saving during normal voyages. No real-time waiting or offline punishment.
- Plan approximately 2–3 minutes between authored points of interest for this chapter. Quiet intervals are intentional, but players should not face a long empty crossing.
- Let conversations reveal relationships aboard ship. The player can defer a conversation without losing required information.
- Preparation changes options: repairs permit a risky route; provisions support a detour; local knowledge reveals an alternative.
- Avoid merely reducing the current global movement constant. Prototype regional distance, camera scale, event spacing, and acceleration together.

## 7. Tactical battle prototype

### Encounter: Bring the passenger boat through

Use a readable 12×16 square water grid as a prototype abstraction. Player controls one armed escort; the passenger vessel follows a selected route. Two hostile craft attempt to intercept it. Vessel choice, armament, and visual models need historical research before final art.

**Win:** the passenger vessel reaches the exit with passengers safe. Disabling all attackers also permits safe passage. Combat should not require sinking every vessel.

**Fail:** the passenger boat is incapacitated. Retry from the pre-battle state with no permanent save loss. Allow the player to revise preparation or use an assisted difficulty setting.

### Round sequence

1. Show wind/current, enemy intended actions, attack arcs, and the passenger route.
2. Allocate three crew teams to helm, guns, and damage control. Multiple teams may reinforce one station.
3. Plan up to two commands for the escort. Show their combined movement, range, resource costs, and predicted environmental drift. Editing the plan is free before committing.
4. Resolve player commands, passenger movement, and enemy actions in a fixed documented order, followed by current effects. A displaced or disabled enemy may lose its forecast action; any fallback is previewed in the plan.
5. Show consequences briefly, then begin the next round. No time limit while choosing.

The first encounter uses deterministic outcomes. Uncertainty comes from opponents and circumstances, not hidden hit-roll frustration. Target roughly 6–10 rounds and 8–10 minutes in a first attempt; tune after observing players.

| Command | Prototype rule |
| --- | --- |
| Maneuver | Costs one command; with helm crew, move up to two cells and change facing once. No helm crew permits only a one-cell emergency move |
| Fire | Costs one command, a guns team, and ammunition; requires a clear broadside arc. Preview hull or rigging damage. At most one shot per round |
| Repair | Costs one command, a damage-control team, and one timber unit; restores a previewed amount of hull, once per round |
| Guard | Costs one command; reduces the next incoming hull hit until the following round |
| Signal | Costs one command; choose the passenger boat's available route or ask it to hold position |
| Parley | One attempt when story evidence is available; Mali exposes the contradictory order, causing one hostile crew to hesitate for a round |

At least one guns team is needed to fire, so assigning every team to speed or repairs has a clear tradeoff. Extra helm crew may extend movement; extra guns crew may increase rigging damage. Keep the bonuses visible and small enough that splitting teams remains useful.

Current arrows produce a previewed one-cell displacement at round end; an anchor/hold stance suppresses drift at the cost of movement. Favorable wind can extend a sailing maneuver; rowing provides a limited alternative so headwinds never trap the player. Grounding consumes movement and disables drift without instant death. Exact values remain tuning variables.

The forecast must make the complete round legible before commitment, including passenger drift. Introduce movement first, then firing, then crew tradeoffs; do not open with every rule at once.

### Why the chapter's choices matter in battle

Niran can identify a usable shallow channel. The sanctuary clue reveals that option early; without it, a safe longer channel remains available. Mali's evidence can earn a temporary opening through parley. Supplies support repair and route choices. Tactical options are earned through the same exploration and dialogue the player already enjoys.

Defer fleet management, crew permadeath, boarding grids, complex ammunition trees, and competitive multiplayer. The later campaign may add two supporting vessels only after one-vessel combat works.

## 8. Sacred exploration: The unlit landing

Use a fictional riverside sanctuary in the prototype, with its final architectural and devotional details reviewed before art production. Do not claim it reconstructs a named holy site.

The landing's keeper needs help recovering travelers' records from a leaking storehouse. Niran secures access from the waterside; Mali compares a damaged entry with a copy kept indoors. Together they discover that the passenger's route was changed before the letter reached town.

Play consists of conversation, spatial observation, and a small document puzzle. Return the records to their keeper. Reward local trust, a navigation clue, and a new conversation aboard ship. Offer a hint and a non-puzzle route to the optional story result.

A lantern across the water may seem to answer a character's private worry; leave its explanation uncertain. This beat works with or without supernatural interpretation. Later sacred journeys should be developed around their own communities rather than reusing this puzzle everywhere.

## 9. Presentation and asset plan

Visual thesis: a busy river world of timber landings, shaded passages, cloth awnings, boats, water reflections, and warm interior pools of light. The reference is a lived-in seventeenth-century environment, not a uniform row of generic shop boxes.

| Asset group | First-chapter deliverable |
| --- | --- |
| Modular environment | One researched kit of foundations, wall bays, roof sections, walkways, docks, doors, and windows |
| Distinctive locations | Customs facade, interpreter's room, working quay, sanctuary, Ayutthaya arrival silhouette |
| Props | Mooring posts, rope, baskets, cargo containers, documents, work tools, lamps, and market goods |
| Vessels | Player river escort, passenger craft, and a reusable hostile variant with readable facing and damage states |
| Characters | Two protagonists with four-direction idle/walk sets; a small supporting NPC cast; portraits for speaking roles |
| Sound | Water, oars, timber, crowds, market activity, restrained music, and clear combat cues |
| Interface | Dialogue, journal, route planner, compact voyage status, and battle command/forecast panels |

Blender is for reusable geometry, UVs, materials, pivots, and GLB exports. Sprites and portraits use the separate 2D pipeline. Establish one approved street-and-interior sample before producing the whole environment kit. Review generated characters for directional consistency, readable silhouettes, feet alignment, and actual transparency.

The existing local Blender background bridge can support authoring, but its presence is not evidence that production Siam assets exist. All new art in this plan remains to be made.

## 10. Build sequence and decision gates

These are delivery milestones, not calendar commitments. Estimate schedules only after the mechanics prototype exposes the actual work.

| Milestone | Concrete output | Gate to the next milestone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Review this design | Resolve tone, opening cast, story boundaries, and minimal combat scope | Owner accepts a coherent first chapter and its exclusions |
| B. Test combat with simple shapes | One encounter, forecast, crew allocation, escort route, retry | A player can explain a useful tactical decision and win through more than one approach |
| C. Test regional travel | Short river scene, three event points, optional stop, acceleration, save/resume | The voyage feels substantial without an empty wait |
| D. Establish the art standard | One Siam street, one interior, one vessel, one complete protagonist sprite set | Visual readability and target-device performance are acceptable |
| E. Assemble the chapter | Both introductions, shared journey, optional sanctuary, battle, arrival, consequences | Entire chapter completes and reloads reliably |
| F. Test with players | Small observed sessions followed by a public demo update | Address navigation confusion, dull travel, tactical ambiguity, and major performance problems |
| G. Expand the campaign | Next protagonist and regional arc | Existing systems support the new story without multiplying unfinished features |

**First implementation task after design review:** the isolated tactical escort encounter. It is the least-proven core system and can be tested before committing to expensive environment art. Keep the existing playable prototype available while this experiment develops.

## 11. Technical integration and verification

Keep Three.js as the renderer and DOM as the interface. Introduce explicit application modes for sailing, on foot, dialogue, battle planning, battle resolution, and menus. Inputs and simulation updates must belong to the current mode only.

Reuse current story transitions, route planning concepts, sprite rendering, town navigation, and saved progression where appropriate. Extract battle and regional-voyage rules into independent serializable modules; do not encode quest flags or crew rules inside meshes. The global geography remains useful for the atlas but cannot stand in for the detailed river.

Future saves need a campaign/version field, active chapter, party, named choices, ship state, regional position, and resolved encounter IDs. Migrate old saves without resetting the existing voyage. Save at safe battle boundaries; a reload resumes the last committed round rather than duplicating rewards or partially executing actions.

Validate the first implementation with:

- Pure-rule checks for legal movement, crew prerequisites, command costs, forecasting, drift, victory/failure, and reward idempotency.
- Complete browser journeys through dialogue, sailing, battle, return to exploration, and save/reload.
- Keyboard and touch operation, readable text, visible focus, and no character hidden behind foreground art.
- Performance measurements on a nominated reference desktop and phone before setting asset budgets. Provisional goals are smooth 60 fps desktop and stable 30 fps mobile; neither is a measured promise yet.
- Observed playtests with roughly five players, including people who did not follow development. Ask them to explain their objective and one consequential choice; watch for repeated confusion rather than treating a small sample as market proof.

## 12. Campaign expansion and scope control

Expand geographically in story-led arcs: Siam first, selected regional ports second, then carefully researched long-distance diplomatic and sacred journeys. Develop the world chart's sense of distance alongside each route's playable content.

Introduce the remaining protagonists progressively. Pairwise chapters establish relationships before the finale. The finale should distribute useful responsibilities—navigation, evidence, negotiation, care, engineering, and resources—rather than giving everyone the same combat role.

Out of scope for the first chapter: a fully rebuilt world, a complete Versailles chapter, the historical 1688 crisis, all six playable protagonists, open-ended supernatural systems, detailed boarding combat, multiplayer, and a new monetization system. The existing Holy Land prototype is exploratory content; its eventual campaign placement and historical treatment remain separate design work.

## 13. Review questions

The draft is ready to discuss around four decisions:

1. Does the pilot/interpreter partnership feel like the right opening, or should another proposed protagonist lead?
2. Is ambiguous folklore the intended tone, or should the campaign commit to grounded history or visible fantasy?
3. Does an escort objective with full turn previews sound more appealing than a primarily real-time battle?
4. Is this concentrated chapter the right first milestone before adding more world regions?

No new game code, assets, speed changes, model setting changes, or publication are part of this planning document.
